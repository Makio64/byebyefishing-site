A practical email safety guide

How to spot a phishing email

A message can look familiar and still ask you to do something risky. Start with the request, then check the sender and destination.

By Makio64 · September 22, 2026

In this guideFive checks before you actThree common examplesWhat to do next

Five checks before you click, pay or reply

  1. Ask whether you expected the request

    A password reset you started is different from an unexpected demand to keep your account open. Urgency alone does not prove a scam, but it is a reason to slow down.

  2. Read the full sender address

    A display name such as “Account Support” can be copied. Check the domain after the @ sign. Even a familiar-looking address is not proof of who sent the message.

  3. Inspect the destination without opening it

    On a desktop browser, hover over a link to view its destination. A familiar word in the address can be misleading: bank.example.attacker.test belongs under attacker.test, not bank.example. These are fictional examples.

  4. Notice what the message asks you to share

    Requests for passwords, sign-in codes, recovery phrases or an unexpected payment deserve an independent check. A padlock or HTTPS connection does not prove the page belongs to the organization you intended.

  5. Verify using a route you already know

    Open the official app, use a saved bookmark or contact the person through an established channel. For a changed invoice, use a previously known phone number rather than the number in the new email.

Three common phishing patterns

The small delivery fee

“Your parcel is waiting. Pay a small fee today.” Check the tracking information through the courier’s official app or your original order.

The account suspension alert

“Confirm your password now or lose access.” Open the service independently and check its account notifications.

The unexpected shared document

A familiar hosting service can contain a malicious page. Ask the sender whether they shared the document before following an unfamiliar sign-in request.

What to do with a suspicious message

Use your mail provider’s phishing-reporting option. Do not reply with private information or forward the message to a public support forum. If you entered a password, open the real account independently and follow the provider’s account-recovery guidance.
